\begin{abstract}
\begin{quote}
Public YouTube data make video-performance prediction deceptively easy: views
strongly reflect channel size, time online and video format, while the platform
does not expose impressions, click-through rate or watch time. On our data, four
non-content variables explain 58.4\% of the variance in log views under
channel-grouped cross-validation. We therefore study a narrower question:
whether observable content distinguishes videos in the top quarter of a peer
group matched on category, channel size, age and format. Our retrospective
dataset contains 1,860 videos with metadata, thumbnails, transcripts, audio and
20 sampled frames; a separate prospective panel records fixed-horizon growth
and near-publication metadata for future evaluation. We encode thumbnails and
all 20 frames with frozen DINOv2, CLIP and ResNet-50 backbones, while title,
description and quality-gated transcripts are encoded separately with
ModernBERT. Structured XGBoost baselines and multimodal representations are
evaluated using repeated channel-grouped development splits. Tuned metadata and schedule
features achieve $0.619\pm0.022$ validation AUC. The best observed content model,
combining field-aware text with CLIP thumbnail and frame aggregates, reaches
$0.613\pm0.044$ and exceeds the structured model on two of five matched
splits. Larger DINOv2 does not help, while frames beat thumbnails. Exact linear-logit
decomposition identifies which modality blocks move individual predictions,
without treating those associations as causes. These results do not
show that content is unimportant; they show that, at this sample size and with
public outcomes, content features provide no reliable incremental prediction
beyond contextual metadata.
\end{quote}
\end{abstract}

%% ==================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%% ==================================================================

Advice to online-video creators commonly attributes performance to thumbnails,
titles, speaking style and editing. Testing those claims from public data is
difficult because the observed outcome is produced jointly by content,
audience size, recommendation exposure and time. A model can therefore predict
views accurately while learning little about the video itself. Such a model is
unsuitable for diagnosis: telling a creator that subscriber count was important
does not identify an actionable property of the upload.

We ask: \emph{after matching videos on observable context, do thumbnail, text,
audio and frame representations predict relative performance beyond metadata?}
The question is important both practically and methodologically. A useful
diagnostic must add information beyond channel and publication shortcuts, and
its evaluation must prevent the same channel from appearing in training and
validation. Public data create a further challenge because impressions,
click-through rate, retention and recommendation exposure are unavailable.

Prior work predicts engagement using content and channel features or studies
large-scale video representations, but often uses absolute outcomes or private
interaction signals. We instead construct a peer-relative binary outcome,
measure the non-content baseline explicitly, and compare engineered features
with frozen visual and language representations.

Our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A cleaned multimodal dataset and an auditable peer-relative label that
      controls category, channel size, video age and format.
\item A channel-grouped evaluation in which every content model is compared
      with the same non-content baseline.
\item Evidence that generic frozen content features do carry signal, but do not
      improve consistently over tuned metadata; controlled visual ablations
      further rule out DINOv2 model size as the main explanation.
\item Exact block explanations showing where predictive similarity fails to
      explain different outcomes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
%% ==================================================================

\subsection{Predicting video performance}
Early-view forecasting establishes a strong but different task. Szabo and
Huberman~\cite{szabo2010predicting} show that early attention predicts later
popularity, and Pinto et al.~\cite{pinto2013early} model the shape of early
YouTube view trajectories. Such observations are powerful predictors but are
available only after release; our question is whether upload content adds
signal without using early views. Khosla et al.~\cite{khosla2014image}
combine image content with social context at scale, motivating our explicit
content-versus-context ablations. Hessel et al.~\cite{hessel2017cats} isolate
content from confounds directly, ranking Reddit posts submitted within seconds
of one another to compare multimodal content against author and timing context
in predicting relative popularity; our peer-relative, within-category label
pursues the same isolation at the cohort level rather than by pairwise timing.

Wu et al.~\cite{wu2018beyond} distinguish engagement from raw popularity by
calibrating watch percentage against video duration. Their formulation is the
closest motivation for our peer-relative outcome, but it requires watch time,
which is unavailable through the public API. Rajaram and
Manchanda~\cite{rajaram2020unboxing} fuse text, audio and visual signals
through an attention-based framework to attribute engagement in influencer
videos. In contrast, our principal question is incremental: whether content
adds predictive value after an explicit metadata baseline, rather than whether
all available variables predict an absolute platform outcome. Liu et
al.~\cite{liu2026survey} survey this convergence toward frozen-encoder,
gradient-boosted multimodal pipelines across the wider popularity-prediction
literature; unlike the work they review, which predicts raw or time-normalized
engagement, we predict a category-relative label so that content signal is
measured net of audience-size confounds such as subscriber count.

\subsection{Multimodal representation}
YouTube-8M~\cite{abu2016youtube} demonstrates large-scale video classification
from temporally aggregated frame and audio features. DINOv2~\cite{oquab2023dinov2}
learns general visual representations without labels, while CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip}
uses image--text supervision and consequently offers a relevant alternative for
text-heavy thumbnails. ModernBERT~\cite{warner2024modernbert} supplies the text
representation. We keep these encoders frozen because the labelled cohort is
small, then compare their features with ResNet-50~\cite{he2016resnet} and with
XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} on engineered metadata. Unlike work targeting
video classification, our output is relative performance and our central test
is whether multimodal content improves over contextual shortcuts. Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2023hierarchical} fuse ResNet-50 image features with text and
structured context attributes for popularity prediction and find context
features alone outperform either content modality in isolation -- the
comparison our metadata baseline is designed to run directly. Lin and
Lee~\cite{lin2024mmf} fuse frozen per-modality encoders through cross-attention
in the 2024 Social Media Popularity Prediction challenge and report that a
single creator's posts carry strongly correlated popularity (79\% with
standard deviation below one), the same leakage risk our channel-grouped
splitting is designed to prevent.

\subsection{Confound control and evaluation protocol}
Rosenblatt et al.~\cite{rosenblatt2024leakage} show, across several forms of
leakage in connectome-based prediction, that covariate correction performed
outside the cross-validation loop biases how much a model appears to explain
beyond confounds, and that grouping related samples across train and test
inflates performance the same way. Our channel-grouped splits and
metadata-only baseline follow the same discipline: whether content explains
virality beyond subscriber-count confounds is evaluated without ever letting a
channel's identity cross a fold boundary.

\subsection{Problem formulation}

For video $v$, let $x_v^{m}$ denote structured metadata and schedule features
and $x_v^{c}$ the available content modalities. We estimate
$p(y_v=1\mid x_v^{m},x_v^{c})$, but the scientific comparison is against
$p(y_v=1\mid x_v^{m})$. Content is useful only if it improves held-out
performance over this structured baseline. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}
shows the selected architecture.

\subsection{The shortcut ceiling}

Before constructing the label, we fit a diagnostic gradient-boosted regressor
to $\log(1+\mathrm{views})$ using only subscriber count, video age, duration and
format. It obtains channel-grouped out-of-fold $R^2=0.584$; random folds increase
this to 0.643. Moreover, retrospective subscriber count may itself have grown
after a successful upload. We consequently treat raw-view prediction as a
measurement of confounding, not as evidence that content has been modelled.

\subsection{Label}

A video is positive if it belongs to the top quarter of a peer cell defined by
category, video-age band, channel-size band and format. Age and size use three
equal-count bands per category; identical rounded subscriber counts remain in
the same band. The resulting $3\times3\times2$ design labels 1{,}854 videos,
23.6\% positive.

Formally, let $c(v)$ denote the cell of video $v$, defined by the tuple
(category, age band, channel-size band, format). Within each cell we rank by
$s(v) = \log(1 + \mathrm{views}(v))$ and label

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
k_c &= \max\!\left(1,\; \lfloor 0.25\,|c| \rfloor\right), \\[2pt]
y(v) &= \mathbf{1}\!\left[\, \mathrm{rank}_{c(v)}\big(s(v)\big) > |c(v)| - k_{c(v)} \right],
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent so each cell contributes approximately one-quarter positives. The log
does not change rank. Three empirical checks determine the configuration. A
30-day minimum-age rule would discard 861 videos and disproportionately remove
Shorts; day-1 and day-5 ranks in the prospective panel already correlate at
0.946, although stability beyond day 5 is not yet known. Without format
stratification, format alone predicts the label at AUC 0.596; stratification
reduces this to 0.447. Finally, four age and size bands create 102 undersized
cells, whereas two bands leave stronger residual confounding. These checks
motivate three bands, separate formats and no age floor.

\subsection{Format leakage}

Portrait framing identifies format for 99.0\% of videos, and raw thumbnail
brightness separates formats at direction-free AUC 0.925. These measurements
could make a visual model appear predictive simply by recognizing Shorts.
Format-stratified labels remove that reward; visual results must therefore be
interpreted as performance beyond this shortcut.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

We use five repeated 60/20/20 channel-grouped development partitions and assert
zero channel overlap within every run. Hyperparameters and decision thresholds
are selected inside each 60\% training partition; the 20\% validation partition
is evaluation-only for that run. However, changing the seed reassigns videos:
every nominal test row appears in training or validation in another run. We
therefore report these results as repeated validation, not an independent test,
and reserve final external evaluation for new prospective videos.

\subsection{Frozen multimodal model}

Visual inputs are encoded by frozen DINOv2 or CLIP transformers; ResNet-50 is a
convolutional control. We compare DINOv2-S and DINOv2-B, centre crop and
aspect-preserving padding, CLS and patch-mean pooling, and the mean representation
of all 20 stored frames. Text uses frozen ModernBERT embeddings. Title,
description and quality-gated transcript are encoded separately; long
transcripts contribute four evenly spaced overlapping chunks. Only 1{,}404
transcripts pass the cleaning gate, and missing-modality masks prevent an absent
transcript from being represented as measured text.

For modality $j$, the late-fusion model computes
$h_j=\mathrm{GELU}(W_jx_j+b_j)$, concatenates the projected representations and
predicts $\hat y=\sigma(W_h[h_1;\ldots;h_J]+b_h)$. We compare this MLP with an
L2-regularised linear probe. Encoder weights remain fixed; regularisation,
architecture and stopping choices are selected using grouped folds inside the
training partition.

For the selected linear fusion, standardised block $\tilde{x}_j$ contributes
exactly $a_j=w_j^\top\tilde{x}_j$ to
$z=b+\sum_j a_j$, with $\hat y=\sigma(z)$. This is a faithful explanation of
the model score, but not evidence that changing a block would change audience
behaviour.

\section{Experiment}

%% ==================================================================

\subsection{Dataset}

\textbf{Retrospective archive.} 1{,}860 already-published videos across four
categories (comedy 464, how-to 373, product reviews 482, vlogs 541), collected
once in August 2026 from 1{,}319 distinct channels. Each video contributes
metadata, a thumbnail, 20 sampled frames, a transcript, and engineered visual and
audio features. This is the only cohort carrying full content modalities and is
therefore what the multimodal model trains on.
The intended 8{,}000-video sample was not reached because YouTube search
pagination returned far fewer retrievable results than its reported totals.

\textbf{Prospective panel.} A live cohort of 12{,}738 newly published videos
(2 September snapshot) records twice-daily growth and first-observed assets.
Its 30-day outcomes mature after submission, so it is reserved for the frozen
external evaluation and contributes no result in this paper.

Frames are sampled non-uniformly: 12 of 20 fall inside the first 60 seconds,
reflecting our design hypothesis that the opening contains disproportionate
information about presentation; the remaining eight cover the rest of the video.

\textbf{Availability.} Code, video identifiers, metadata and reproducible
derived tables are available at \texttt{github.com/Adamex-SVK/yt-diag}. Raw
videos and extracted imagery are not redistributed because reuse permission was
not established for most source videos.

\subsection{Pre-processing}

\textbf{Transcripts.} YouTube's automatic captions use rolling display windows,
so adjacent timed cues contain overlapping text. We reconstruct transcripts from
the timed cues before encoding them. In a 113-video audit sample this reduced
the median repeated-8-gram ratio from 0.349 to 0.000; reconstructed word counts
were 35.2\% of the naively concatenated counts. Transcript provenance then
separates 944 native-English and 467 translated-English files from 449 sparse,
wrong-language or missing files, which are withheld from the encoder.

\textbf{Colour temperature.} RGB samples are averaged in linear light, mapped
through the D65 sRGB-to-CIE-XYZ transform, and compared with a Planckian-locus
lookup table in CIE 1960 UCS space. Estimates farther than $D_{uv}=0.05$ from
the locus are treated as missing rather than assigned a misleading temperature.
This yields valid thumbnail CCT for 1{,}854 videos and valid estimates for
36{,}652/37{,}200 frames.

\subsection{Descriptive analysis}

The dataset is not a balanced collection of interchangeable modalities.
Regular videos have median duration 843 seconds compared with 37 seconds for
Shorts. Usable transcript coverage is 92.4\% for regular videos but 60.2\% for
Shorts. Subscriber count has the strongest training-partition association with
log views (Spearman $\rho=0.705$). Duration illustrates why pooled summaries are
unsafe: its view association is $-0.048$ overall but $0.250$ within regular
videos and $0.095$ within Shorts. The 88 acoustic features are also highly
redundant, with 23 pairs satisfying $|\rho|>0.95$. Consequently, results are
reported by format where sample size permits, and missingness is retained as
information rather than handled through listwise deletion.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

We report AUC-ROC as the primary metric because it is threshold-free and
invariant to the class balance, which differs slightly between cells. For a
scoring function $f$ over positives $\mathcal{P}$ and negatives $\mathcal{N}$,

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}||\mathcal{N}|}
\sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}}
\mathbf{1}\!\left[f(x_i) > f(x_j)\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent i.e.\ the probability that a randomly chosen top-quartile video is scored
above a randomly chosen typical one; 0.5 is chance. We report PR-AUC alongside
it because roughly 24\% of videos are positive, and average precision is the
more honest summary under imbalance. $F_1 = 2PR/(P+R)$; finalist thresholds are
chosen inside the corresponding training partition.

\subsection{Limitations}
%% ==================================================================

\begin{itemize}
\item Labels are a cohort-relative convention, not ground truth. Across 16
      configurations only 83 of 438 ever-viral videos are viral in all of them.
\item Band edges and cutoffs are computed over the whole cohort before splitting,
      making the labels transductive by construction.
\item Frame extraction for non-CC videos sits outside YouTube's terms of service;
      only 7 of 1{,}860 videos carry a Creative Commons licence.
\item External factors --- platform promotion, timing, luck --- cap achievable
      accuracy and are unobservable to us.
\item Product reviews are a different population (median 9{,}953 views against
      comedy's 1{,}254{,}042), an artefact of the category-mapping workaround.
\item A post-freeze ablation found that audio, isolated from visual, exceeds
      tuned metadata+schedule XGBoost ($0.632\pm0.028$ vs.\ $0.614\pm0.022$;
      see Results). This was discovered after the 2026-09-03 model freeze, is
      not part of the frozen finalist, and inherits the same overlapping-split
      caveat as every other repeated-validation number in this report --- it
      is a reported finding, not a change to the frozen comparator.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
%% ==================================================================

Public view counts make it easy to build a predictor that mostly recognizes
channel scale and publication context. After controlling those factors through
a peer-relative label and channel-grouped evaluation, tuned metadata+schedule
XGBoost reaches $0.619\pm0.022$ validation AUC. Frozen content representations
are informative but do not improve reliably on that comparator: the best
observed CLIP, text and metadata fusion reaches $0.613\pm0.044$ and wins two of
five matched splits. Controlled ablations show that all-frame CLIP features are
the strongest visual representation, while ResNet-50 remains weak and the larger
DINOv2 encoder does not improve the full fusion. Engineered visual features, and
audio when bundled with them, likewise failed to improve the tuned structured
comparator; isolating audio alone in a post-freeze ablation did improve it
(Results, Limitations), a finding not yet incorporated into the frozen policy.
The result is therefore a limitation on what can be
diagnosed from this retrospective public dataset, not evidence that content has
no effect on viewers. Exact attribution shows that visual blocks move the
linear model, but a matched error pair shows that predictive explanations are
not causal diagnoses.
Fixed-horizon prospective outcomes are needed to separate content from
post-publication edits and platform exposure.
