How's it going? I have the new HO scale
Golden Gate Depot Southern Pacific
coach, lounge, diner, and observation
car. These plastic models represent the
prototype found all over the Southern
Pacific Railroad from around the 1910s
until the 1980s.
Let's start by unboxing the car. All of
the cars have the same style packaging.
The label on one end is the models label
and on the other end is the Golden Gate
Depot's information.
All boxes came with a plastic wrap
protecting the outside.
Each car is protected with a plastic
cradle wrapped in a plastic sheet.
The unboxing is straightforward and none
of the cars come with spare parts.
However, the diner does come with a
spare step detail part in case one
breaks on the model.
This isn't my first time taking this car
out of the box. As you can see, the
coupler trip pin is cut. I'll go over
that later on in the video.
A good look at the underbody detail with
some separately applied detail and some
molded parts, but overall looks really
good. And before we deep dive, let's
look at what Golden Gate Depot
manufactured and some of the history of
the prototype. Golden Gate Depot did a
great job with providing most if not all
of the paint schemes that were used on
these prototypes. This includes, but not
limited to the dark olive and SP and SP
lines, daylight, solid gray, two-tone
gray, golden state, UP yellow, up
two-tone, SP stainless with scarlet
stripe, and some of the war
advertisements for the Navy, Army, and
Marines. The SP 72 ft inner urban steel
coach was built around 1923 by the
Pullman Company. Southern Pacific wanted
to replace their aging fleet of wooden
pasture cars. Their first order was 60
coaches seating about 96 passengers
each. Some people may refer to these as
Harammans, but technically that's not
right. They are identified as suburbans
or subs. These cars were 72 ft in
length, but coupler to coupler just a
little bit over 80 ft.
From their first order in 1923 to the
last one in 1927, SP had a total of 75
Suburban cars. The suburban heavyweight
coaches were primarily used along the
San Francisco commute service throughout
its entire life, but can also be seen on
trains such as the Suntan and various
excursions. The years that each paint
scheme operated are SP Lions dark olive
from 1923 to 1946, SP dark olive 1946 to
1954, two-tone gray starting in 1954,
and solid gray in 1959 until 1985 when
these cars retired. Now, on to the
model. We are in Bakersfield, California
at the La Mesa Model Railroad Museum in
San Diego, California. Here's a close-up
view of the interior. The windows do
appear a little bubbly, better than the
Heramman run from a few years ago, but
still noticeable. I will be replacing
these windows with clear styrene in the
future. The interior is well lit and
that has capacitors in there to ensure
flicker-free operation. The end of the
car has a nice steamline connection and
air hose details. For the diaphragms,
although they are good in size, I'm sad
to see that they are not operable. I
feel that a car of this price should be
having working diaphragms.
As I mentioned before, I had to cut the
coupler trip pins because some of them
were hitting the switches from being too
low of a coupler height. The couplers
are held in a spring mechanism to allow
these cars to perform on tight radius
turns. The air hose and steamlight
connection is the piece that holds the
coupler box in place. Just like the
Haramman run, these cars roll
exceptionally well. Some of the best
rolling passenger cars ever made. Add to
that, with these cars being very
lightweight, any engine can pull
multiple of these cars with ease.

A little history about this observation.
This is a 72 TAC O TAC1 observation car
specifically lettered Central Pacific or
CP.
These were converted into diners by the
1930s. All of them. So post 1930s these
cars did not exist.
I wanted to show how the coupler spring
system works. The idea behind this
system is to extend the coupler when on
a tight radius turn to compensate for
the non-working diaphragms and to ensure
smooth operation. This system causes the
gap between the cars to spread apart
when pulling a load and is visually
unappealing. Notice how far the coupler
extends when I pull on them. On a
straight section of track, the coupler
extends far out, causing the coupler to
look crooked.
This can be clearly shown as an example.
Here I'm running the train in reverse
and notice how tight the diaphragms are
with each other. This looks really good.
In the forward direction, I'm going to
stop the video on the first two suburban
cars. The first car is pulling 11 cars.
Notice the gap between car one and two.
Going towards the end of the train, look
at the gap between cars 11 and 12. If
these coupler boxes were screwed in to
the frame, we wouldn't have this issue.
